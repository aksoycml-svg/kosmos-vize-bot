The '''Mexican free-tailed bat''' or '''Brazilian free-tailed bat''' ('''''Tadarida brasiliensis''''') is a medium-sized [[bat]] native to [[North America|North]], [[Central America|Central]], and [[South America]] and the [[Caribbean]], so named because its tail can be almost half its total length and is not attached to its [[uropatagium]]. It has been claimed to have the fastest horizontal speed of any animal, reaching level flight speed of up to {{convert|160|km/h|disp=flip|abbr=on}}.<ref>{{Cite web|url=https://www.batcon.org/press/speedy-bat-smashes-speed-record/|publisher=[[Bat Conservation International]]|website=batcon.org|title=Speedy Bat Smashes Speed Record|date=November 9, 2016|access-date=October 20, 2023}}</ref> It also flies the highest among bats, at altitudes around {{convert|3300|m|ft|abbr=on}}.<ref name="High Altitude Flights of the Free-T">{{cite journal|jstor=1379076|doi=10.2307/1379076|title=High Altitude Flights of the Free-Tailed Bat, ''Tadarida brasiliensis'', Observed with Radar|journal=Journal of Mammalogy|volume=54|issue=4|page=807|year=1973|last1=Williams|first1=T. C.|last2=Ireland|first2=L. C.|last3=Williams|first3=J. M.}}</ref>

It is regarded as one of the most abundant [[mammal]]s in [[North America]]. Its proclivity towards roosting in huge numbers at relatively few locations makes it vulnerable to [[habitat destruction]] in spite of its abundance. For instance, up to 1.5 million bats reside under [[Congress Avenue Bridge|just one bridge in Austin]]. The [[Texas Legislature]] recognized the Mexican free-tailed bat as the "Official Flying Mammal" of the State of Texas in 1995.<ref>{{cite web | title=74(R) SCR 95 Senate committee report - Bill Text | url=https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/74R/billtext/html/SC00095S.htm }}</ref>

== Taxonomy ==
The Mexican free-tailed bat was [[species description|described]] as a new species in 1824 by French zoologist [[Isidore Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire]]. Geoffroy placed it in the now-defunct genus ''Nyctinomus'' with a binomial name of ''Nyctinomus brasiliensis''.<ref>{{cite journal| url=https://biodiversitylibrary.org/page/5752603| pages=342–343| journal=Annales des sciences naturelles |last=Geoffroy| first=I.| year=1824|volume=1| title=Par M. Isidore Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire. Lu á la Société d'histoire naturelle de Paris le vendredi 5 mars 1824| trans-title=By Mr. Isidore Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire. Read at the Natural History Society of Paris on Friday, 5 March 1824|language=fr}}</ref>
The [[holotype]] had been collected in [[Brazil]].<ref>{{cite book| title=Systematic Results of the Study of North American Land Mammals to the Close of the Year 1900| first1=Gerrit| last1= Smith Miller| first2= James A. G.| last2= Rehn| year=1901|publisher=Society of Natural History|page=[https://archive.org/details/systematicresult00mill/page/271 271]| url=https://archive.org/details/systematicresult00mill}}</ref>
Molecular sequence data indicate ''T. brasiliensis'''s closest relatives are the [[sister species]] ''[[Sauromys petrophilus]]'' of [[South Africa]] and ''[[Tadarida aegyptiaca]]'' of Africa and South Asia. These three species form a [[clade]] believed to be about 18 million years old.<ref>{{cite journal|last1=Ammerman|first1=LK|last2=Lee|first2=DN|last3=Tipps|first3=T|title=First molecular insights into the evolution of free-tailed bats in the subfamily Molossinae (Molossidae, Chiroptera)|journal=Journal of Mammalogy|date=2012|volume=93|issue=1|pages=12–28|doi=10.1644/11-mamm-a-103.1|doi-access=free}}</ref>

== Physical description ==
Mexican free-tailed bats are typically {{convert|9|cm|in|abbr=on}} in length and weigh around {{convert|7-12| g|oz|abbr=on}} with females tending to be slightly heavier than males by 1–2 grams for increased fat storage to use during gestation and nursing.<ref name="Wilkins 1989"/> 
Their tails are almost half their total length and stretch beyond the [[uropatagium]], giving them the name "free-tailed" bats. Their ears are wide, rounded, and large compared to their heads, nearly meeting at the front of the face, but distinctly not joined at the midline and projecting anterodorsally from just before the muzzle to the back of the head. They use their large ears to help them find prey using [[Animal echolocation|echolocation]]. ''T. brasiliensis'' is distinguished among North American'' Tadarida'' bats in possessing deep wrinkles on the upper lip and in having a Z-shaped upper third molar, which is used for grinding insects. These individuals possess canines which are larger in males than in females.<ref name="Wilkins 1989"/> The wings are elongated and narrow with pointed tips, making them well-equipped for quick, straight flight patterns. Their fur color ranges from dark brown to gray. The Mexican free-tailed bat's large feet have distinct long, white bristles.<ref>{{cite web |url=http://www.nsrl.ttu.edu/tmot1/tadabras.htm |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20000511033326/http://www.nsrl.ttu.edu/tmot1/tadabras.htm |archive-date=11 May 2000 |title=Brazilian Free-tailed Bat (Tadarida brasiliensis)}}</ref> These bristles, namely those on digits one and five and the toes, as well as on the edge of the [[uropatagium]], are [[Photoluminescence|photoluminescent]] under [[ultraviolet]] light. The function of this trait, if any, is unknown.<ref>{{Cite journal |last1=Gual-Suárez |first1=Fernando |last2=Ramos-H. |first2=Daniel |last3=García |first3=Falco |last4=Pérez-Montes |first4=L. Ernesto |last5=Narro Delgado |first5=Alonso |last6=Medellín |first6=Rodrigo A. |date=2024-08-08 |title=Ultraviolet-induced photoluminescent bristles on the feet of the Mexican free-tailed bat (Tadarida brasiliensis) |url=https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s42991-024-00441-3 |journal=Mammalian Biology |volume=104 |issue=6 |pages=751–756 |language=en |doi=10.1007/s42991-024-00441-3 |issn=1618-1476|url-access=subscription }}</ref>
[[File:Mexican free-tailed bat (8006850693).jpg|thumb|Showing the bristles (photo by [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]])]]
The dental formula of ''Tadarida brasiliensis'' is {{DentalFormula|upper=1.1.1.3|lower=3.1.2.3}}.<ref name="Reid 2006">{{cite book|last=Reid| first= F.| date= 2006| title= A field guide to mammals of North America, north of Mexico| volume= 4| publisher= Houghton Mifflin Harcourt| page=136}}</ref>

== Range and ecology ==
[[File:Free-tailed bats.jpg|thumb|left|alt=The image depicts hundreds of bats flying at dusk|Bats flying near Frio Cave in Concan, Texas]]
The Mexican free-tailed bat ranges from the southern half of the continental United States through most of Mexico, and through most of Central America into South America. Their range in South America is less understood where they live in the eastern Brazilian highlands and coast, the northeastern Andes, the coast of Peru and northern Chile, and the center of [[Argentina]].<ref name="Wilkins 1989" /> They are absent in much of the Amazon rainforest. They are also found in the Caribbean, and are native to all of the Greater Antilles and 11 of the Lesser Antilles.<ref>Baker, R. J., Genoways, H. H. (1978). "Zoogeography of Antillean bats", pp. 53–97 in ''Zoogeography in the Caribbean'', F. B. Gill (ed.). Philadelphia: Acad</ref> The largest known colony is found at [[Bracken Cave]], north of [[San Antonio]], Texas, with nearly 20 million bats; the bats from this colony congregate in huge numbers at altitudes between {{convert|180|and|1,000|m|ft|abbr=on}}, and even as high as {{convert|3000|m|ft|abbr=on}}.

=== Habitat ===
Mexican free-tailed bats roost primarily in caves. However, they also roost in buildings of any type as long as they have access to openings and dark recesses in ceilings or walls.<ref name="Wilkins 1989">{{cite journal|author=Wilkins, K. |year=1989|title=''Tadarida brasiliensis'' |journal=Mammalian Species|issue=331|pages= 1–10|doi=10.2307/3504148 |jstor=3504148}}</ref> The bats can make roosting sites of buildings regardless of "age, height, architecture, construction materials, occupancy by humans and compass orientation".<ref name="Wilkins 1989" /> Caves, though, need to have enough wall and ceiling space to fit millions of bats.<ref name="Wilkins 1989" /> Before buildings, free-tailed bats in the Southeastern United States probably roosted in the hollows of trees such as red mangrove, black mangrove, white mangrove, and cypress. However, most bats in Florida seem to prefer buildings and other man-made structures over natural roosts.<ref name="Wilkins 1989" /> Caves in Florida tend to be occupied mostly by the [[southeastern myotis]]. Caves in Florida tend to have pools of water on the floor and the free-tailed bats do not need as much relative humidity as the southeastern myotis.<ref name="Wilkins 1989" />

=== Migration ===
[[File:Tadarida brasiliensis outflight Hristov Carlsbad Caverns.jpg|thumb|alt=The image depicts hundreds of bats flying at dusk|Mexican free-tailed bats, emerging from [[Carlsbad Caverns]], [[Carlsbad Caverns National Park]], New Mexico]]
Mexican free-tailed bats in southeastern Nevada, southwestern Utah, western Arizona, and southeastern California come together to migrate southwest to southern California and [[Baja California Peninsula|Baja California]].<ref name="Wilkins 1989" /> Bats in southeastern Utah, southwestern Colorado, western New Mexico, and eastern Arizona travel through the western edge of the [[Sierra Madre Oriental]] into Jalisco, Sinaloa, and Sonora. Some bats that summer in Kansas, Oklahoma, eastern New Mexico, and Texas migrate southward to South Texas<ref name ="Glass 1982">{{cite journal|author=Glass BP |year=1982|title= Seasonal movements of Mexican free-tail bats ''Tadarida brasiliensis mextcana'' banded in the Great Plains|journal= Southwestern Nat.|volume=27|issue=2|pages=127–133|doi=10.2307/3671136|jstor=3671136 |bibcode=1982SWNat..27..127G }}</ref> and Mexico.<ref name="Wilkins 1989" /> Some bat populations in other areas of North America do not migrate, but are residents and may make seasonal changes in roost sites.<ref name="Wilkins 1989" /> While this migration is extremely extensive, a way must exist for these bats to continuously congregate in the same roosts every year. ''T. brasiliensis'' bats have copious numbers of sebaceous glands covering their entire bodies. These glands leave a trace of a lasting scent to which other bats are sensitive. This odor is crucial to marking habitual roosts.<ref>{{Cite journal|last=Glass|first=Bryan P.|date=1982|title=Seasonal Movements of Mexican Freetail Bats Tadarida brasiliensis mexicana Banded in the Great Plains|journal=The Southwestern Naturalist|volume=27|issue=2|pages=127–133|doi=10.2307/3671136|jstor=3671136 |bibcode=1982SWNat..27..127G }}</ref>
[[File:Austin - bats watching 3.jpg|thumb|alt=The image depicts hundreds of bats flying from under the Congress Avenue Bridge at dusk|Dusk emergence of bats at the Congress Avenue Bridge in Austin, Texas]]Bats ranging eastward from East Texas do not migrate, but local shifts in roost usage often occur seasonally.<ref name="Wilkins 1989" /> Also, a regional population that ranges from Oregon to California, has a year-round residence.

=== Diet ===
Mexican free-tailed bats are primarily insectivores. They hunt their prey using echolocation. The bats eat [[moth]]s, [[beetle]]s, [[dragonfly|dragonflies]], [[Diptera|flies]], [[Hemiptera|true bugs]], [[wasp]]s, and [[ant]]s. They usually catch flying prey in flight.<ref>{{cite journal|doi=10.1644/1545-1542(2005)86[599:VIDOTM]2.0.CO;2 |year=2005 |volume=86 |issue=3 |pages=599–605 |title=Variation in Diet of the Mexican Free-Tailed Bat (''Tadarida brasiliensis mexicana'') |journal=Journal of Mammalogy |last1=McWilliams |first1=Lisa A. |s2cid=86220457 |doi-access=free }}</ref> Large numbers of Mexican free-tailed bats fly hundreds of meters above the ground in Texas to feed on migrating insects.<ref>{{cite journal|pmid=21669777|year=2008|last1=McCracken|first1=G. F.|title=Brazilian free-tailed bats (Tadarida brasiliensis: Molossidae, Chiroptera) at high altitude: Links to migratory insect populations|journal=Integrative and Comparative Biology|volume=48|issue=1|pages=107–18|last2=Gillam|first2=E. H.|last3=Westbrook|first3=J. K.|last4=Lee|first4=Y. F.|last5=Jensen|first5=M. L.|last6=Balsley|first6=B. B.|doi=10.1093/icb/icn033|doi-access=free}}</ref> The consumption of insects by these bats can be quite significant.<ref>Corrigan, Robert. [http://www.texasmosquito.org/Bats.html Do Bats Control Mosquitoes?] {{Webarchive|url=https://web.archive.org/web/20150402152305/http://www.texasmosquito.org/Bats.html |date=2 April 2015 }} texasmosquito.org</ref><ref name="desertmuseum.org">[http://www.desertmuseum.org/kids/bats/mexican_free_tailed_bat.php Animal Fact Sheet: Mexican Free-Tailed Bat]. [[Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum|Desert Museum]].</ref> The bat colony in Austin, Texas eats about 30,000 pounds of insects each night.<ref name=":0">{{Cite web |last=Dudek |first=Dani |date=April 2, 2023 |title=10 Astounding Facts About The Legendary Bats Of Austin |url=https://austin.com/10-astounding-facts-about-the-legendary-bats-of-austin/}}</ref>

The onset of evening emergence from caves and the end of returns at dawn tend to correlate with sunset and sunrise, respectively, with dawn returns ending increasingly later in correlation to sunrise throughout the summer season. Reproductive females tended to emerge earlier in the evening and return later at dawn to gather the extra nutrients they require for reproduction and offspring care.<ref name="Lee 2001">{{cite journal|last1=Lee| first1= Y. F.| last2= McCracken| first2= G. F.| date= 2001| title= Timing and variation in the emergence and return of Mexican free-tailed bats, Tadarida brasiliensis mexicana| journal= Zoological Studies| volume= 40| issue=4| pages= 309–316| url=http://zoolstud.sinica.edu.tw/Journals/40.4/309.pdf}}</ref> ''T. brasiliensis'' bats spend around 60% of their active time foraging while aerial, mostly hunting at heights of {{convert|6|-|15| m| ft|abbr=on}}.<ref name="Wilkins 1989"/> Individuals will fly {{convert|50|km|mi|abbr=on}} in one night to reach foraging areas. The loose, wrinkled skin around the mouth is thought to aid in expanding the mouth during flight to catch insects. ''T. brasiliensis'' requires free water sources to maintain water balance: individuals from the arid environment of New Mexico tend to have thicker [[renal]] tissue layers compared to ''T. brasiliensis'' from the less arid California, revealing that urine concentrating abilities and water use varies geographically with aridity.<ref>{{Cite journal|last=Bassett|first=JE|date=1982|title=Habitat aridity and intraspecific differences in the urine concentrating ability of insectivorous bats.|journal=Comparative Biochemistry and Physiology|volume=72 | issue = 4 |pages=703–708|doi=10.1016/0300-9629(82)90152-9|pmid=6126307}}</ref>

=== Health and mortality ===
One individual bat was recorded to have lived eight years, based on dentition.<ref>{{cite book|last1=Gannon|first1=M.|first2=A.|last2=Kurta|first3=A.|last3=Rodriquez-Duran|first4=M.|last4=Willig|year=2005|title=Bats of Puerto Ricoo: An Island Focus and a Caribbean Perspective|location=Jamaica|publisher=The University of the West Indies Press|isbn=976-640-175-6}}</ref>{{page number needed|date=June 2024}}  Predators of the bat include birds such as [[greater roadrunner]]s, [[red-tailed hawk]]s, [[American kestrel]]s, [[great horned owl]]s, [[American barn owl|barn owl]]s, and [[Mississippi kite]]s.<ref name="Wilkins 1989" /><ref>{{cite journal|last1=Mikula|first1=P.|last2=Morelli|first2=F.|last3=Lučan|first3=R. K.|last4=Jones|first4=D. N.|last5=Tryjanowski|first5=P.|year=2016|title=Bats as prey of diurnal birds: a global perspective|journal=Mammal Review|volume=46|issue=3|page=160|doi=10.1111/mam.12060 |bibcode=2016MamRv..46..160M |hdl=2318/2164750|hdl-access=free}}</ref> Other predators include [[common opossum]]s, [[American hog-nosed skunk]]s, [[striped skunk]]s, [[raccoon]]s, [[Pantherophis obsoletus|black rat snake]]s, [[Masticophis flagellum|coachwhip]]s, [[Agkistrodon contortrix|copperhead]]s, [[Micrurus fulvius|coral snake]]s, and occasionally [[Lepomis cyanellus|green sunfish]].<ref name="Wilkins 1989" />

[[White-nose syndrome]] (WNS) caused by infection by the fungus ''[[Pseudogymnoascus destructans]]'' has increased in prevalence since 2006, mostly affecting species of bats that roost underground such as the [[little brown bat]]. The fungus, now suspected to have spread from accidental transportation by human cave workers, is thought to cause frequent arousals during bat hibernation, causing an individual to use fat stores much more quickly and die of starvation before the end of winter. WNS can affect ''T. brasiliensis'', but has yet to be greatly introduced to their habitat due to their preference for more arid caves. WNS has low prevalence in the subtropical and tropical regions where ''T. brasiliensis'' resides.<ref>{{Cite journal|last=Fenton|first=B|date=2012|title=Bats and white-nose syndrome|journal=PNAS|volume=109 | issue = 18 |pages=6794–6795|doi=10.1073/pnas.1204793109|pmc=3344961|pmid=22517743|bibcode=2012PNAS..109.6794F|doi-access=free}}</ref>

Certain chemicals in the fur of these bats have antimicrobial activity against common mammalian skin pathogens. The chemical [[nonanal]] is found in such a concentration that its antimicrobial activity can inhibit the growth of two fungal pathogens that cause ringworm (''[[Trichophyton mentagrophytes]]'' and ''[[Trichophyton rubrum|T. rubrum]]''), as well as ''[[Malassezia furfur]]'', a [[yeast]]. Two other common skin disease-causing microbes that are inhibited by the hair's concentration of nonanal include ''[[Staphylococcus aureus]]'' and ''[[Cutibacterium acnes]]''.<ref>{{cite journal | last1 = Wood | first1 = William F. | last2 = Szewczak | first2 = Joseph M.   | year = 2007 | title = Volatile Antimicrobial Compounds in the Pelage of the Mexican Free-tailed Bat, Tadarida brasiliensis mexicana | journal = Biochemical Systematics and Ecology | volume = 35 | issue = 9 | pages = 566–568 | doi=10.1016/j.bse.2007.04.002| bibcode = 2007BioSE..35..566W }}</ref>

== Behavior ==
[[File:Versatility-and-Stereotypy-of-Free-Tailed-Bat-Songs-pone.0006746.s006.ogv|thumb|A male displays and sings in the presence of females ([[:File:Versatility-and-Stereotypy-of-Free-Tailed-Bat-Songs-pone.0006746.s007.ogv|watch in slow motion]]).]]
Mexican free-tailed bats are nocturnal foragers and begin feeding after dusk. They travel 50&nbsp;km in a quick, direct flight pattern to feed. This species flies the highest among bats, at altitudes around {{convert|3300|m|ft|abbr=on}}.<ref name="High Altitude Flights of the Free-T"/> Bats appear to be most active in late morning and afternoon between June and September.<ref>{{cite journal|doi=10.2307/1381461 |jstor=1381461 |title=Natural History of the Brazilian Free-Tailed Bat in the San Luis Valley of Colorado |journal=Journal of Mammalogy |volume=68 |issue=2 |page=224 |year=1987 |last1=Svoboda |first1=P. The species has been measured at a [[ground speed]] of {{convert|160|km/h|abbr=on}}, measured by an aircraft tracking device.<ref name=McCracken>{{cite journal |author1=McCracken, Gary F. |author2=Safi, Kamran |author3=Kunz, Thomas H. |author4=Dechmann, Dina K. "Since they did not measure winds at the place and time where the bats are flying, one can therefore not exclude that the top speeds are not bats flying in a gust."}}</ref>

=== Guano and ammonia ===
Among bats that roost in great, concentrated numbers, ''T.'' ''brasiliensis'' roosts produce large quantities of urine and [[guano]]; from 22 to 99 metric tons per cave and over 18,700 metric tons are produced annually. The concentrated waste generates high levels of toxic ammonia in the air of a cave. ''T. brasiliensis'' individuals have genetic adaptation for withstanding or countering these high levels of ammonia. The bats' content of CO<sub>2</sub> and protein in respiratory mucus and CO<sub>2</sub> dissolved in blood plasma increase with increasing levels of dissolved ammonia, providing the bats with a buffer against pH change. This allows the bats to filter out a large majority of inhaled ammonia before it reaches toxic levels in the blood. ''T. brasiliensis'' bats are thought to swarm in spiraling motions within caves to ventilate ammonia and renew the air.<ref name="Wilkins 1989"/> The guano is used to manufacture eco-friendly<ref name=":0" /> fertilizers and insecticides<ref name=":0" />.  In the Civil War, Confederate troops used bat guano to produce saltpeter, a key ingredient in gunpowder.<ref name=":0" /> 

=== Echolocation ===
Mexican free-tailed bats use [[Animal echolocation|echolocation]] for navigation and detecting prey. Traveling calls are of a brief but constant frequency. However, they switch modulated frequency calls between 40 and 75&nbsp;kHz if they detect something.<ref name="Gillam 2007">{{cite journal|doi=10.1016/j.anbehav.2006.12.006|title=Variability in the echolocation of ''Tadarida brasiliensis'': Effects of geography and local acoustic environment|journal=Animal Behaviour|volume=74|issue=2|page=277|year=2007|last1=Gillam|first1=Erin H.|last2=McCracken|first2=Gary F.|s2cid=53174117}}</ref> Typically, the frequency range of their echolocation is between 49 and 70&nbsp;kHz, but can be between 25 and 40&nbsp;kHz if something crosses their path while in flight.<ref name="Gillam 2007" />

On 6 November 2014, Aaron Corcoran, a biologist at [[Wake Forest University]], North Carolina, reported online in ''[[Science (journal)|Science]]'' that his team and he had detected Mexican free-tailed bats emitting ultrasonic vocalizations that had the effect of jamming the echolocation calls of rival bats hunting moths. Using these 'jamming' calls, one bat could reduce another bat's chance of catching a prey insect and then attempt to catch that insect itself. Earlier researchers had discovered some 15 types of social calls made by Mexican free-tailed bats and reported that they could adjust their calls to avoid interfering with others in range of their calls.<ref>{{cite web |last= Morell |first= Virginia |title= Holy blocked bat signal! Bats jam each other's calls |url=https://www.science.org/content/article/holy-blocked-bat-signal-bats-jam-each-others-calls|access-date=8 November 2014 |website=new.sciencemag.org |date=6 November 2014}}</ref><ref>{{cite web |last= Hogenboom |first= Melissa |title= Bats sabotage rivals' senses with sound in food race |url= https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/science-environment-29931995|access-date=8 November 2014 |publisher=BBC News |date=7 November 2014}}</ref>

=== Mating and reproduction ===
[[File:Tadarida brasiliensis - Bahamas - Long Island (sic) - Cartwright Cave - March 2006 - 2.jpg|thumb|alt=The image depicts bats hanging from the wall of a cave|Free-tailed bats roosting at a cave in the Bahamas]]
During the breeding season, females aggregate into maternity roosts. The size of these roosts depends on the environment, with caves having the larger roosts. Mating can occur in an aggressive or passive form. In the aggressive form, the male controls the female's movements, keeping her away from the other bats in the roost.<ref name="Keely 2004">{{cite journal|doi=10.1644/BME-004|title=The Mating System of ''Tadarida brasiliensis'' (Chiroptera: Molossidae) in a Large Highway Bridge Colony|journal=Journal of Mammalogy|volume=85|pages=113–119|year=2004|last1=Keeley|first1=Annika T. H.|last2=Keeley|first2=Brian W.|doi-access=free}}</ref> He also tends to vocalize when mating. During passive copulation, the males simply flies to a female in her roost and quietly mounts her with no resistance. This species is a [[Promiscuity#Other animals|promiscuous]] breeder and both sexes copulate with multiple partners.<ref name="Keely 2004" /> Females become sexually mature at about 9 months, while males take even longer, at two years. Females enter [[estrus]] once a year, which typically lasts five weeks in the spring. The gestation period of the bat lasts 11–12 weeks, with only one young being born. A number of pups are left in "creches", while their mothers roost elsewhere. The female uses vocalizations and scent to identify her pup. The mother imprints her scent on the young early on.<ref>{{cite journal|doi=10.2307/1382150|jstor=1382150|title=Factors Influencing Female-Pup Scent Recognition in Mexican Free-Tailed Bats|journal=Journal of Mammalogy|volume=72|issue=3|page=624|year=1991|last1=Loughry|first1=W. J.|last2=McCracken|first2=G. F.}}</ref> However, young try to steal a suckle from any female that passes through the cluster. Through examining the genotypes of female-pup nursing pairs sampled from colonies in Texas, it has been measured that nursing is partially selective along genetic lines, with approximately 17% of mothers nursing pups that were not their offspring, ostensibly due to the difficulty of consistently locating and selectively nursing their own pups in extremely large colonies.<ref>{{Cite journal|last=McCracken|first=Gary F.|date=1984-03-09|title=Communal Nursing in Mexican Free-Tailed Bat Maternity Colonies|url=https://www.science.org/doi/abs/10.1126/science.223.4640.1090|journal=Science|volume=223|issue=4640|pages=1090–1091|doi=10.1126/science.223.4640.1090|pmid=17830157|bibcode=1984Sci...223.1090M|s2cid=206572086|url-access=subscription}}</ref> A mother will nurse her young daily, and by 4–7 weeks old they are full grown, fully weaned, and independent.<ref>{{cite journal|doi=10.2307/1382746|url=http://www.bu.edu/cecb/files/2009/08/postnatalmexican.pdf|jstor=1382746|title=Postnatal Growth and Development in the Mexican Free-Tailed Bat (''Tadarida brasiliensis mexicana''): Birth Size, Growth Rates, and Age Estimation|journal=Journal of Mammalogy|volume=76|issue=3|page=769|year=1995|last1=Kunz|first1=Thomas H.|last2=Robson|first2=Simon K.}}</ref>

== Conservation ==
Though abundant and widespread, some local populations have prompted protection and conservation efforts. For instance, during the spring and summer, one of the largest Mexican free-tailed bat populations inhabits Cueva de la Boca, a cave near [[Monterrey]], Mexico. In 2006, the Mexican environmental conservation NGO, [[Pronatura Noreste]], purchased the property. Because of a reduction by more than 95% of the original 20 million bat population to 600,000, as a result of vandalism, pollution, and uncontrolled tourism, the organization decided to buy the property to place it under conservation. Other species of high ecological value that inhabit the cavern are also being protected. 
Similarly, [[Bat Conservation International]] bought [[Bracken Cave]] from private ownership in 1992. BCI planned to revert any land changes that were a result of farming or ranching in order to conserve the biodiversity and wildlife there. Preserving the land around Bracken Cave was important, as it is home to the world's largest bat colony, and any human encroachment would be detrimental to their population. BCI also protects the land from artificial light pollution, which can distress the bats.<ref name="TPWDBrackenCave">{{cite web |title=Bracken Cave Preserve – Texas Hill Country Region |url=https://tpwd.texas.gov/huntwild/wild/species/bats/bat-watching-sites/bracken-cave-preserve.phtml |website=Texas Parks and Wildlife Department |access-date=24 September 2020}}</ref><ref name="BCIBrackenCave">{{cite web |title=Preserving Bracken Cave and the largest bat colony in the world |url=https://www.batcon.org/our-work/protect-restore-landscapes/bracken-cave-preserve/ |website=batcon.org|publisher=Bat Conservation International}}</ref>

=== Economic importance ===
In [[Austin, Texas]], a colony of Mexican free-tailed bats summers (they winter in [[Mexico]]) under the [[Congress Avenue Bridge]] 10 blocks south of the [[Texas State Capitol]]. It is the largest urban colony in North America, with an estimated 1,500,000 bats.<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.batcon.org/home/index.asp?idPage=122 |title=Congress Avenue Bridge |work=Congress Avenue Bridge Bat Colony |url-status=bot: unknown |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20081112012632/http://www.batcon.org/home/index.asp?idPage=122 |archive-date=12 November 2008 }}</ref> Each night they eat {{convert|10000|to|30000|lb|abbr=on}} of insects. Each year, they attract 100,000 tourists who come to watch them. In [[Houston]], Texas, a colony is living under the Waugh Street Bridge over [[Buffalo Bayou]]. It is the home to 250,000 bats and also attracts viewers. The Mexican free-tailed bat is the official flying mammal of the state of Texas,<ref>{{cite web|url=http://www.tsl.state.tx.us/ref/abouttx/symbols.html|title=Texas State Symbols|work= Texas State Library and Archives Commission}}</ref> as well as Oklahoma.<ref>{{Cite web |date=2006-03-08 |title=Senate Selects Bat as States Flying Mammal {{!}} Oklahoma Senate |url=https://oksenate.gov/press-releases/senate-selects-bat-states-flying-mammal |access-date=2023-11-06 |website=oksenate.gov |language=en}}</ref>

Hundreds of tons of guano were mined in Texas annually between 1900 and 1980. In the early 20th century, bat guano was Texas' largest mineral export, before oil. In 1863, a gun powder factory opened near San Antonio, the saltpeter of which was provided by local guano mines.<ref>{{cite journal|url=https://www.batcon.org/article/the-lives-of-mexican-free-tailed-bats/|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20230401132056/https://www.batcon.org/web/20230401132056/https://www.batcon.org/article/the-lives-of-mexican-free-tailed-bats/|archive-date=April 1, 2023|publisher=Bats Conservation International|journal=Bats Magazine|volume=12|issue=3|year=1995|first=Merlin D.|last=Tuttle|title=The lives of Mexican free-tailed bats}}</ref>
