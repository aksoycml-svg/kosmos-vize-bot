The weapon that burned its own base: the bat bomb

During World War II, Lytle Adams, a dentist from Pennsylvania, got an idea for a weapon from the bats of Carlsbad Caverns and wrote to the White House in January 1942. President Roosevelt passed the letter on with a note: "This man is not a nut." Louis Fieser, the chemist who developed napalm, built tiny incendiary capsules to be glued to bats; chilled into sleep and packed into bomb-shaped canisters, the bats would wake in mid-air, roost under roofs and set Japan's wooden cities on fire. On May 15, 1943, at the Carlsbad auxiliary air base in New Mexico, armed bats were accidentally released; they roosted under a fuel tank and the test range burned. By some accounts hangars, barracks and a general's car burned too; those details could not be confirmed from a primary source. The project then moved to the Navy and the Marine Corps and, by one account, was tested on replica Japanese houses at Dugway, Utah. About $2 million is said to have been spent. Admiral King cancelled it on hearing it would not be ready before mid-1945; some sources suggest priority had by then shifted to the atomic bomb. It was never used in the war.
