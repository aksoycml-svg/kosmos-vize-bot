The photographer who fit Paris's mail on a pigeon's tail

In September 1870, Prussian armies surrounded Paris and cut the telegraph; roughly two million people lost contact with the outside world. Mail could leave by balloon, but balloons drifted with the wind and could not come back. Replies came in by pigeon: pigeons were carried out of the city by balloon and, once released, flew home to their lofts. Because a bird can carry very little, photographer René Dagron shrank letter pages onto finger-sized film; the film was tied to the tail feather, projected onto a wall in Paris and copied by hand. Since this had to be done in Tours, where the government sat, Dagron himself had to leave Paris by balloon. As the story is told, another balloon that lifted off with his from the Gare d'Orléans on the morning of 12 November 1870 came down under Prussian fire; Dagron's climbed, landed in Prussian territory, and he escaped and reached Tours on 21 November. The details of his escape rest largely on Dagron's own account, and sources differ: some say his balloon dumped ballast and got out of range, others that it was hit and came down in enemy territory. Figures vary by source too: messages that reached Paris are given as roughly 60,000 to 150,000, the balloon count is about 66, and the siege is given as four to four and a half months; sources disagree on how many birds made it back, and agree only that most did not.
