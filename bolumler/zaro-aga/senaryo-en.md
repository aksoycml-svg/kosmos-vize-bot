## 0. Up front

**Turn line:** Before: Americans come to watch a porter whose passport makes him a hundred fifty-six; the porter jokes to reporters that the first hundred years are the hardest. / After: an insurance statistician claims he died at ninety-seven; if so, he never finished those "hardest" first hundred years. Type: **reversal** (the opening joke comes back as the possible truth; number vs. man).

**Hero:** Zaro Agha, a porter from Istanbul. The viewer follows his hands, his face and the folded paper in his pocket.

**Main question (stated out loud):** was Zaro Agha really a hundred fifty-six? P07: doctors say "a hundred twenty at most, not a hundred fifty-six". P09–P10: "ninety-seven" meets "a hundred fifty-six" in back-to-back sentences.

**Five-line spine**
1. **Situation:** a porter from Istanbul whose passport shows a very old birth year, making him a hundred fifty-six.
2. **Want:** to have his paper believed (inferred from his showing it to the doctors; single source, hedged). The promoter's want: money.
3. **Obstacle:** while he is shown as "the world's oldest man", doctors read his bones and lower the number; his paper convinces no one.
4. **Change:** the number leaves his hands and changes with whoever holds it: 156 in the passport, 120 at most for the doctors, 157 on the death certificate, 160 on the gravestone.
5. **Outcome:** an insurance statistician says 97. If so, Zaro Agha never finished the hundred years he called the hardest. His real age is still unknown.

**Last line (written first):** "If the statistician was right, the porter whose passport said a hundred fifty-six never finished those 'hardest' first hundred years."
Setups: "porter", "passport", 156 in P01 · birth year P03 · the quote P05 (18.6–25.0; about 3 s past the first third, because the invitation bridge in P04 was added) · "insurance statistician" P09. The last line repeats part of the quote so the callback is recognisable.

**Tone:** no mockery. The claim always belongs to the paper ("whose passport"), never presented as Zaro Agha's lie. The joke is his own.

**Written for the ear (tur-1g):** every sentence names who did what to whom; situation before event. Nothing was shortened by dropping a subject; events were removed instead.

### Sound off
Screen texts as in §4. Reads: a porter from Istanbul, 156 by his passport, is invited and shown in American circus tents; doctors say 120 at most; he slaps down his paper; after his death 157, 160, then 97; nobody knows.

### Sound on (narration only; the text for the cold listener)
1. Americans came to watch a porter whose passport made him a hundred fifty-six.
2. The porter was Zaro Agha, from Istanbul.
3. Zaro Agha's passport showed a very old birth year.
4. A promoter reportedly invited Zaro Agha to America to make money.
5. In New York, reporters quoted Zaro Agha: "The first hundred years are the hardest."
6. The promoter showed Zaro Agha in circus tents as "the world's oldest man".
7. But doctors who X-rayed Zaro Agha's bones reportedly said a hundred twenty at most, not a hundred fifty-six.
8. Furious, Zaro Agha showed the doctors the paper with his birth year.
9. After Zaro Agha died, an insurance statistician claimed Zaro Agha had really died at ninety-seven.
10. If the statistician was right, the porter whose passport said a hundred fifty-six never finished those "hardest" first hundred years.

Remaining risks, stated plainly: sentence 9 still gives no basis for the 97, because the sources give none; sentence 7 leaves "years old" implied after two age numbers; sentence 10 names the hero as "the porter", not by name, to return in the hook's words.

## 9. Publishing
- **Cover:** THE "156-YEAR-OLD" PORTER (3 words)
- **Description:** Zaro Agha is said to have worked for many years as a porter in Istanbul, later for the municipality. When he reached New York in 1930 his passport made him 156, and the press quoted him, through an interpreter, saying "the first hundred years are the hardest." For months he was shown in circus tents as "the world's oldest man," and in 1931 he was taken to Europe as well. Who invited him is uncertain; reportedly an American promoter. In New York he was hit by a car, and X-rays found no fracture. The doctors' "120 at most" and his showing a birth certificate rest on a single source. He died in Istanbul in 1934 and was autopsied despite his relatives' objections. His death certificate is reported to say 157 and his gravestone 160; in 1939 an actuary claimed he had died at about 97. Birth records of the time were unreliable, and his real age is unknown. Scenes, the number sign and gestures are dramatized. Voiceover is AI-generated. Sources: Wikipedia (Zaro Aga), Wikimedia Commons archive photos, period press records.
