## 0. Spine

**Turn:** Before: the drummer is taken for dead and lies in the morgue; the conductor has fifteen players left / After: the same drummer is on stage, and the whole city is listening. Type: **reversal.**

**Hero:** conductor Karl Eliasberg, the single hero. Aydarov is his drummer; the narration names the link in P06, P07 and P12.

1. **Situation:** the German army has surrounded Leningrad; Eliasberg has about fifteen musicians left.
2. **Want:** to perform Shostakovich's new symphony. It needs nearly a hundred.
3. **Obstacle:** hunger. The drummer Aydarov collapses; Eliasberg hears he is dead.
4. **Change:** Eliasberg goes down to the morgue to say goodbye and sees Aydarov's finger move. Aydarov recovers and returns.
5. **Result:** the lights come on, loudspeakers carry the concert to the city and toward the German lines, and Aydarov is among the drummers.

**Last line (written first):** "The orchestra played to the end. Among the drummers was Aydarov, the man from the morgue."

## 1. Accuracy notes (EN-specific)
- "the story goes" in narration (P06); source label on screen (P08). No "list of the dead", no "corpse cart".
- Who said he was dead is not known from the sources, so the sentence is built on Eliasberg: "Eliasberg heard Aydarov was dead."
- Finger, not breath: the finger variant is the majority one. The breath variant is in the caption.
- "drummer / drummers", never "snare drum".
- "about fifteen", "nearly a hundred", "hundreds of thousands". Artillery shot removed for length. No Astoria banquet, no German-soldier quote.
- "toward the German lines so the enemy would hear" (the broadcast was aimed there for psychological effect; we do not claim it was heard).
- No sourced reason for why the symphony had to be played in Leningrad, so none is narrated; on-screen text notes it was written in the besieged city.

## 8. Publishing
- **Cover:** THE DRUMMER FROM THE MORGUE (5 words)
- **Caption:** On 9 August 1942, in Leningrad under German siege, conductor Karl Eliasberg led Shostakovich's Seventh Symphony. Of the radio orchestra's 40 players, about 15 were left in the city; the rest were sent from the front and from military bands. The concert was broadcast by loudspeaker across the city and toward the German lines. The story of percussionist Zhavdet (Jaudat) Aydarov comes from the research of historian Yulia Kantor and from his grandson Nadim Aydarov; details differ between tellings (in most the conductor sees a finger move, in others he hears him breathe). Aydarov is reported to have lived until 2000. In 1964 the surviving players met again in the same hall, with instruments placed on the chairs of those who had died. Scenes and gestures are dramatised; no recording of the symphony is used. Narration is AI-generated. Sources: Wikipedia (Leningrad première, Karl Eliasberg, Siege of Leningrad), St. Petersburg Philharmonia, Y. Kantor.
